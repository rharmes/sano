Essential Nepali phrases with Romanized pronunciations for English speakers

Word Pronunciation Meaning Usage
Basics
Namaste nuh-muh-stay Hello / Goodbye This is the most common greeting in Nepal, used at any time of day. It's accompanied by pressing your palms together in front of your chest. The word literally means "I bow to the divine in you" and is appropriate in both formal and informal settings.
Namaskaar nuh-mus-kaar Hello (formal) More formal version of "namaste." Both mean essentially the same thing and are used as greetings. "Namaskaar" might be used in very formal or official settings.
Hajur huh-joor Yes (polite) / Pardon? This versatile word shows respect and can mean "yes," "I agree," or "pardon me?" depending on context and tone. It's considered more polite than simply saying "cha" (yes) and is commonly used when speaking to elders or in formal situations.
Ho hoh Yes (informal) Nepalis may also shake their head side-to-side for yes, which can confuse visitors at first!
Hoina HOY-nah No (informal)
Hola ho-laa Maybe Used to express possibility or uncertainty. This is a casual, conversational way to say "maybe" or "perhaps." It can be used when you're not sure about something or when you want to leave options open. For example, if someone asks if you'll come to dinner, you might say "hola" if you're uncertain.
Maaf garnuhos maaf gar-noo-hos Excuse me / I'm sorry This phrase is used to apologize or to get someone's attention politely. It's appropriate in both casual and formal settings. You can use it when you need to pass by someone, when you've made a mistake, or when you need someone's attention.
Kripaya kri-puh-yuh Please This formal word for "please" is used to make polite requests. It's especially common in written communication and formal speech. In casual conversation, people often omit "kripaya" and rely on polite verb forms instead.
Dhanyabad dhun-ya-baad Thank you This is the standard way to express gratitude. It's used in both casual and formal situations. While Nepali culture doesn't emphasize saying thank you as much as Western cultures, it's still appreciated and understood universally.
Swagat cha swaa-gut chuh You're welcome Standard response to "dhanyabad." "Swagat" means welcome. Shows politeness and acknowledges the thanks. You might also hear "kei pardaina" (it's nothing/no problem).
Subha prabhat shoo-bha pruh-bhaat Good morning A formal morning greeting. "Prabhat" means morning or dawn. However, "namaste" is more commonly used throughout the day, including mornings. "Subha prabhat" is more formal and literary.
Subha ratri shoo-bha raa-tree Good night Used when saying goodbye at night or before going to sleep. "Subha" means good or auspicious, and "ratri" means night. It's a warm way to wish someone well before parting in the evening.
Huncha hoon-chuh Okay / It will be done This is one of the most useful words in Nepali, expressing agreement, acceptance, or acknowledgment. You'll hear it constantly in everyday conversation. It can mean "okay," "alright," "it's possible," or "it will happen."
Hudaina hoo-dye-nuh Not okay / It won't work This is the negative form of "huncha," used to express that something is not acceptable, not possible, or won't work. It's a polite way to decline or say no without being too direct.
Ke bhayo? kay bha-yo What happened? Used to ask what's wrong or what occurred. If someone seems upset or if something unusual happened, this is an appropriate question. "Ke" means what, and "bhayo" means happened.
Kehi chhaina keh-hi chhai-nuh Nothing / It's nothing A polite response when asked what's wrong or if you need anything. "Kehi" means something/anything, and "chhaina" means "is not." It's a way to reassure someone that everything is fine.
Timi ti-mi You (informal) The informal "you" used with friends, family, children, or people younger than you. Unlike "tapai" (formal you), "timi" shows familiarity and closeness. Use it only with people you know well or who are younger.
Cha chuh Yes / Is / Has A simple affirmative word meaning "yes" or serving as the verb "to be" or "to have." It's one of the most frequently used words in Nepali. For example, "ramro cha" (it is good) or "paisa cha?" (do you have money?).
Chhaina chhai-nuh No / Is not / Don't have The negative form of "cha." It means "no," "is not," or "don't have." Used frequently in daily conversation. For example, "paisa chhaina" means "I don't have money" or "there is no money."
Dherai ramro dheh-rye rum-ro Very good / Excellent An emphatic way to say something is very good or excellent. "Dherai" intensifies "ramro." Use this to express strong approval or satisfaction with food, experiences, or outcomes.
Ekdum ramro ek-dum rum-ro Very good / Excellent Strong expression of approval or satisfaction. "Ekdum" is an intensifier meaning "very" or "extremely." More emphatic than just "ramro" alone.
Sarai ramro suh-rye rum-ro Very good / Really good Another way to emphasize something is very good. "Sarai" means "very" or "really." Interchangeable with "ekdum ramro" to express strong positive feelings.
Introductions
Tapai ko naam ke ho? tuh-pie-ko naam kay ho What is your name? (formal) This is the polite way to ask someone's name. "Tapai" is the formal "you," "ko" makes it possessive, and "naam" means name. It's appropriate when meeting someone for the first time in a formal or respectful context.
Hajur ko naam ke ho? huh-joor ko naam kay ho What is your name? (very polite) An extremely respectful way to ask someone's name, more formal than "tapaiko naam ke ho." Used when addressing someone of very high status, significantly older, or in very formal situations.
Timro naam ke ho? tim-ro naam kay ho What is your name? (informal) The informal way to ask someone's name using "timro" (your - informal). Use this with friends, children, or people younger than you. It establishes a casual, friendly tone.
Mero naam ___ ho meh-ro naam ___ ho My name is ___ Use this phrase to introduce yourself. "Mero" means "my," "naam" is "name," and "ho" is "is." Simply insert your name in the blank space. This works in both formal and informal situations.
Tapai lai bhetda khushi lagyo tuh-pie lie bhet-daa khu-shi lug-yo Nice to meet you A polite phrase when meeting someone for the first time. "Bhetda" means "meeting," and "khushi lagyo" means "felt happy." It's a formal way to express pleasure at making someone's acquaintance.
Tapai lai kasto cha? tuh-pie lie kus-toh chuh How are you? (formal) This is the polite way to ask someone how they're doing. Use "tapai" when addressing someone older, someone you don't know well, or in professional settings. The response is typically "Sanchai cha" (I'm fine).
Sanchai chu sun-chay choo I'm fine This is the standard response when someone asks how you are. "Sanchai" means fine or well, and "cha" is the verb "to be." You might also hear "ramro cha" (it's good) as a similar response.
Kaha jane? kuh-haa jaa-nay Where are you going? This is a common casual greeting in Nepal, similar to "How are you?" in English. It's not always a literal question about your destination. A common response is to state where you're going or simply say "tira tira" (around/here and there).
Tapai Nepali bolnu hunchha? tuh-pie nay-paa-li bowl-noo hoon-chuh Do you speak Nepali? A polite way to ask if someone speaks Nepali. "Tapai" is the respectful "you," "bolnu hunchha" means "do you speak." You can replace "Nepali" with "English" (Angreji) or other languages.
Ma Nepali bolchhu muh nay-paa-li bowl-choo I speak Nepali Use this to indicate you can speak Nepali. You might add "ali ali" before "Nepali bolchhu" to say you speak only a little. This phrase helps set expectations for communication.
Ma Nepali boldina muh nay-paa-li bowl-di-nuh I don't speak Nepali The negative form, indicating you don't speak Nepali. This can be useful for tourists or new arrivals. Often people will still be helpful and try to communicate through gestures or simple words.
Tapai kaha bata ho? tuh-pie kuh-haa buh-taa ho Where are you from? A common question when meeting someone new. "Bata" means "from," so this literally asks "from where are you?" You can answer with your country or city name followed by "bata ho."
Ma ___ bata ho muh ___ buh-taa ho I am from ___ Insert your country or city in the blank to answer where you're from. For example, "Ma America bata ho" (I am from America) or "Ma Kathmandu bata ho" (I am from Kathmandu).
Kaha basnu huncha? kuh-haa bus-noo hoon-chuh Where do you live? Polite way to ask where someone lives. "Basnu huncha" is the respectful form of "to live/stay." You can respond with your neighborhood, city, or country.
Ma ___ ma baschu muh ___ maa bus-choo I live in ___ Response to where you live. Insert your location in the blank. "Ma" means I, and "baschu" means "I live." For example, "Ma Kathmandu ma baschu."
Pheri bhetaula pheh-ri bheh-tow-laa See you again / Let's meet again A friendly way to say goodbye with the expectation of meeting again. "Pheri" means again, and "bhetaula" means "let's meet." It's warmer than a simple goodbye and shows you value the relationship.
Ramrari jaanu ram-rah-ree jaa-noo Go safely / Take care A caring farewell wishing someone a safe journey or safe travels. "Sanchai" means safe/well, and "jaanu" means to go. Shows concern for someone's wellbeing.
Comprehension
Maile bujhe moy-lay boo-jhay I understand / I understood This phrase indicates that you've understood what someone said. "Bujhe" is the past tense form meaning "understood." You can also say "ma bujhchu" (I understand - present tense) for ongoing understanding. This is very useful when someone is explaining something to you and you want to confirm your comprehension.
Maile bujhina moy-lay boo-jhi-nuh I don't understand This essential phrase lets others know you haven't understood. "Ma" means "I" and "bujhina" is "don't understand." It's helpful when learning the language or when someone is speaking too quickly.
Thaha chhaina tuh-haa chhai-nuh I don't know Indicates you don't know something. "Thaha" means knowledge, and "chhaina" means "is not" or "don't have." A straightforward way to admit you lack information about something.
Malai thaaha cha muh-lie tuh-haa chuh I know The opposite of "thaha chhaina," indicating you do know something. "Malai" means "to me" or "I," and "thaha cha" means "have knowledge." Shows understanding or awareness.
Sochna parcha soch-nuh pur-chuh Need to think / Let me think Indicates you need time to consider something. "Sochna" means "to think," and "parcha" means "is needed." A polite way to delay giving an immediate answer.
Ali bistari bolnuhos ah-lee bis-taa-ri bowl-noo-hos Please speak slowly Use this when you need someone to slow down their speech. "Bistari" means slowly, and "bolnuhos" is the polite command form of "to speak." This is particularly useful for language learners.
Yo ke ho? yo kay ho What is this? A simple and useful question for identifying objects or learning new vocabulary. "Yo" means "this," "ke" means "what," and "ho" is "is." You can also say "tyo ke ho?" for "What is that?"
Kasari? kuh-saa-ri How? A question word asking about the manner or method of doing something. It's useful when you need instructions or want to understand the process of something. People use it frequently when learning new tasks.
Kina? ki-nuh Why? Asks for the reason behind something. While useful, be mindful that repeatedly asking "kina" might be seen as challenging authority in more traditional or formal settings, especially with elders.
Nepali ma ___ lai ke bhanchha? nay-paa-li maa ___ lie kay bhaan-chuh How do you say ___ in Nepali? Essential phrase for language learners. Insert the English word in the blank space. "Nepali ma" means "in Nepali," "lai" is a particle, "ke bhanchha" means "what do you say/call." For example, "Nepali ma 'apple' lai ke bhanchha?" (How do you say 'apple' in Nepali?). Very useful for building vocabulary.
Lekhera dinuhola? lay-khay-raa di-noo-ho-laa Can you write that down? A polite request asking someone to write something for you. "Lekhera" means "writing" or "having written," "dinuhola" means "can you give/do." This is particularly helpful when learning new words, getting addresses, or when you need a written reference of something spoken.
Pheri bhannus pheh-ri bhan-noos Can you repeat that? Polite way to ask someone to repeat what they just said. "Pheri" means "again," and "bhannus" or "bhandinuhos" means "please say." Use this when you didn't hear clearly or need to hear something again to understand better. The second version is slightly more formal.
Bujhnubhayo? / Bujyau? booj-noo-bha-yo / booj-yoo Do you understand? Used to check if someone has understood what you said. "Bujhnubhayo" is the polite/formal version, while "bujhyo" is more casual. Teachers, hosts, and anyone explaining something will use this phrase to ensure comprehension. You can respond with "bujhe" (I understood) or "bujhina" (I didn't understand).
Tapai Nepali bolnu hunchha? tuh-pie nay-paa-li bowl-noo hoon-chuh Do you speak Nepali? A polite way to ask if someone speaks Nepali. "Tapai" is the respectful "you," "bolnu hunchha" means "do you speak." You can replace "Nepali" with "English" (Angreji) or other languages.
Ma Nepali bolchhu muh nay-paa-li bowl-choo I speak Nepali Use this to indicate you can speak Nepali. You might add "ali ali" before "Nepali bolchhu" to say you speak only a little. This phrase helps set expectations for communication.
Ma Nepali boldina muh nay-paa-li bowl-di-nuh I don't speak Nepali The negative form, indicating you don't speak Nepali. This can be useful for tourists or new arrivals. Often people will still be helpful and try to communicate through gestures or simple words.

Ma ... bolchhu muh ... bowl-choo I speak English
Meals
Paani dinus paa-ni di-noos Please give me water A practical phrase for restaurants or homes. "Paani" is water and "dinus" is the polite form of "give me." You can replace "paani" with other items you need.
Khana khanu bhayo? kha-na kha-noo bha-yo Have you eaten? This is a common way of showing care and concern in Nepali culture, similar to "How are you?" in English. It's not always a literal inquiry about whether someone has eaten, but rather a friendly greeting showing you care about their wellbeing.
Chiya khaane? chi-yaa khaa-nay Will you have tea? Common offer of hospitality. Note that Nepalis say "eat tea" (chiya khaane) rather than "drink tea." Tea is an integral part of Nepali culture and this offer is made frequently.
Khana khaeko chu kha-na khaa-ay-ko choo I've eaten / I have eaten Past tense statement indicating you've already eaten. "Khaeko" is the past participle of "to eat," and "chu" means "I am/have." Use this when someone asks if you've eaten or offers you food when you've already had a meal. It's a polite way to decline additional food.
Khana khaeko chhaina kha-na khaa-ay-ko chhai-nuh I haven't eaten Indicates you have not yet eaten. "Chhaina" is the negative form meaning "have not" or "is not." Use this when someone asks if you've eaten and you're still hungry. In Nepali culture, asking "khana khanu bhayo?" (have you eaten?) is a common way of showing care, and this is the honest response if you haven't.
Kasto cha? kus-toh chuh How is it? Asks about the quality, condition, or state of something. Can refer to food, weather, experiences, or situations. A versatile question used frequently in conversation.
Mitho cha mi-toh chuh It's delicious Use this to compliment food. "Mitho" means delicious or sweet (in taste), and expressing appreciation for food is an important part of Nepali hospitality. Your host will be pleased to hear this.
Pugyo poog-yo Enough / That's sufficient This word indicates you've had enough, whether it's food being served, items being poured, or any other quantity. It's polite but firm. In restaurants, servers might keep offering more food, and this word stops them.
Pardaina pur-dye-nuh No need / Not necessary Politely declines something or indicates something isn't necessary. Less direct than saying "no." For example, when someone offers something you don't need, you can say "pardaina, dhanyabad."
Thik cha teek chuh It's okay / It's fine A versatile phrase expressing that something is acceptable, correct, or fine. "Thik" means right, correct, or okay. You can use it to agree with someone, to say something is acceptable, or to indicate you're doing fine.
Khana mitho cha kha-na mi-toh chuh The food is delicious Past tense compliment for food. "Thiyo" is the past tense of "was." Use this after finishing a meal to thank your host or compliment the chef.
Ma machha-maasu khadina muh mah-cha maa-soo kha-di-nuh I don't eat meat Important phrase for vegetarians. "Maasu" means meat, and "khadina" means "don't eat." This clearly communicates dietary restrictions. You can also say "ma shakahari ho" (I am vegetarian) for a more general statement. Nepal has a large vegetarian population, so this dietary preference is well understood and respected.
Ma dudh, dahi ra ghue khadina - TODO muh doodh, da-hee raa ga-yoo kha-di-nuh I don't eat milk and milk products "Dudh" means milk, "ra" means and, "banaeko kura" means "made things/products." You might also learn "paneer" (cheese), "dahi" (yogurt), and "ghee" (clarified butter) to identify specific items to avoid.
Khana tayar bhayo kha-na tuh-yaar bha-yo The food is ready Announces that a meal is prepared and ready to eat. "Tayar" means ready or prepared, and "bhayo" means "became" or "is." Family members often use this to call others to the table. You might also hear "khana khana aaunu" (come to eat) as an invitation to the meal.
Household Living
Charpi kaha cha? chur-pi kuh-haa chuh Where is the bathroom? Essential question for any household. "Charpi" is the common Nepali word for bathroom/toilet (you may also hear "toilet"). "Kaha cha" means "where is." This is often one of the first questions you need to ask in a new place. The response will typically include directions like "tyaha" (there) with a gesture.
Ma nuhaauna jane muh noo-hau-nuh jaa-nay I'm going to bathe Announces your intention to shower or bathe. "Nuhaauna" means "to bathe/shower," and "jane" means "going." This is polite to say in a household so others know the bathroom will be occupied.
Ma kehi maddat garna sakchu? muh keh-hi mud-dut gar-nuh suk-choo Can I help with anything? Polite offer to assist with tasks. "Kehi" means "anything/something," "maddat" means "help," and "garna sakchu" means "can I do." This shows respect and willingness to contribute to household work. It's especially important in a host family situation to offer help with cooking, cleaning, or other chores.
Ma bhada maajhchu muh bhaan-daa maaj-choo I'll wash the dishes Offers to do the dishes. "Bhada" (or "bhanda") means dishes/utensils, and "maajhchu" means "I will wash/clean." This is a helpful phrase for contributing to household chores. In many Nepali homes, guests are not expected to wash dishes, but offering shows good manners and appreciation. Your host may decline the offer, but it's polite to make it.
Purchasing
Kati ho? kuh-ti ho How much is it? Essential for shopping and bargaining, this phrase asks about price or quantity. "Kati" means "how much" or "how many." Shopkeepers will understand this immediately and tell you the price.
Mahango cha muh-hun-go chuh It's expensive Use this when you think the price is too high. Bargaining is common in Nepal's markets, and saying something is expensive might lead to a lower price. "Mahango" means expensive and "cha" means "is."
Sasto cha sus-toh chuh It's cheap The opposite of mahango, this phrase indicates something is inexpensive or affordable. "Sasto" means cheap or inexpensive. You can use this to compliment a good price or good value.
Kati ma? kuh-ti maa For how much? Asks about price or cost. "Ma" in this context means "for." Used when bargaining or asking about prices. Shopkeepers expect this question in markets.
Kam garnuhos kum gar-noo-hos Please reduce / Make it less Used when bargaining to ask for a lower price. "Kam" means less or reduce. Bargaining is common in Nepali markets, and sellers often expect some negotiation.
Antim mol kati? un-tim mol kuh-ti What's your final price? Asks for the best/final price. "Antim" means final or last, and "mol" means price. Use this near the end of bargaining to get the seller's best offer.
Aru ke chaahiyo? uh-roo kay chaa-hi-yo What else do you need? Shopkeepers often ask this after you've purchased something. "Aru" means other or else, "ke" means what, and "chaahiyo" means needed. You can say "aru kehi chhaina" (nothing else).
Aru kehi chhaina uh-roo keh-hi chhai-nuh Nothing else Indicates you don't need anything else. "Kehi" means something/anything. A polite way to decline further offers from shopkeepers or hosts.
Time
Kahile? kuh-hi-lay When? A simple question word asking about time. You can use it to ask when something will happen, when someone will arrive, or when an event occurred. It's useful in planning and making arrangements.
Aaja aa-juh Today Refers to the current day. You'll hear this often in daily conversation when discussing plans or events. It can be combined with other words like "aaja nai" (today itself, meaning right away).
Bholi bho-li Tomorrow Refers to the next day. Interestingly, "bholi" is also used colloquially to mean "later" or "in the future," and Nepal has a reputation for "bholi culture" where things are often postponed until tomorrow.
Hijo hi-jo Yesterday Refers to the previous day. This word is commonly used when recounting recent events or experiences. It's a straightforward time marker in everyday conversation.
Navigation
Kaha? kuh-haa Where? A basic question word asking about location. It's essential for getting directions, finding places, or asking where things or people are. You can combine it with other words to form more specific questions.
Yaha yuh-haa Here Indicates this location or place. Use it when pointing out where something is or where you are. It's often accompanied by a gesture to indicate the specific spot being referenced.
Tyaha tyuh-haa There Indicates that location or place, somewhere away from the speaker. It's used for pointing to locations that aren't immediately near. You can use it when giving or receiving directions.
Kata janey? kuh-taa jaa-nay Where to go? Taxi drivers commonly ask this when you get in. It's a shortened, casual form asking where you want to go. You can respond with your destination or say "tira" (that way) while pointing.
Roknus rok-noos Please stop Useful in taxis or buses when you want the vehicle to stop. "Rok" means to stop, and "nus" makes it polite. You can also say "yaha roknus" (stop here) to indicate you want to get off at this location.
Bato dekhaunus baa-toh dekh-ow-noos Please show me the way A polite request for directions. "Bato" means road or way, and "dekhaunus" means "please show." People in Nepal are generally helpful with directions and will often go out of their way to help.
Toilet kaha cha? toy-let kuh-haa chuh Where is the toilet? A practical necessity. In Nepal, you might also hear "charpi" as another word for toilet. This question will be understood everywhere and people will happily point you in the right direction.
Descriptors
Ramro rum-ro Good / Nice / Beautiful A versatile adjective describing something positive. It can describe food, weather, people, experiences, or objects. "Ramro cha" means "it's good" and is a common way to express satisfaction.
Naramro nuh-rum-ro Bad / Not good The opposite of ramro, this word describes something negative or unpleasant. "Na-" is a common prefix in Nepali that negates words. It can describe poor quality, bad behavior, or unfortunate situations.
Thulo thoo-lo Big / Large Describes size or importance. It can refer to physical size or to someone's age or status. "Thulo manchhe" means "big person" and can refer to someone important or an adult.
Sano saa-no Small / Little The opposite of thulo, indicating something small in size. "Sano bacha" means small child. It can also indicate something minor or insignificant in context.
Dherai dheh-rye Many / Much / Very Indicates a large quantity or high degree. "Dherai dhanyabad" means "thank you very much." It's also used to say there's a lot of something. "Dherai manchhe" means many people.
Ali ali uh-li uh-li A little bit Indicates a small amount or degree. When learning Nepali, you might say "ma ali ali Nepali bolchhu" (I speak a little Nepali). The repetition emphasizes the smallness of the quantity.
Weather
Kasto chha hawa? kus-toh chha huh-waa How's the weather? Asks about weather conditions. "Hawa" means air or weather. You can respond with descriptions like "ramro cha" (it's good), "garmi cha" (it's hot), or "pani pariracha" (it's raining).
Pani pariracha paa-ni puh-ri-ruh-chuh It is raining A useful phrase for discussing weather. "Pani" means water, and "pariracha" is the present continuous form of "to fall." Monsoon season makes this a frequently used phrase in Nepal.
Garmi cha gur-mi chuh It's hot Describes hot weather or temperature. "Garmi" means heat. In Nepal's hot season (before monsoon), this phrase is commonly used. You might also hear "dherai garmi cha" (it's very hot).
Jado cha jaa-do chuh It's cold Used to describe cold weather, especially during winter months in Nepal. "Jado" means cold or winter. In mountainous areas, you'll hear this frequently during the colder seasons.
Medical
Kripaya malaai maddat garnuhos kri-puh-yuh muh-lie mud-dut gar-noo-hos Please help me A polite request for assistance. "Malaai" means "to me," and "maddat garnuhos" means "please help." This is useful in emergencies or when you need someone's assistance with something.
Sahayog garnuhos suh-haa-yog gar-noo-hos Please cooperate / Please help Formal request for cooperation or assistance. "Sahayog" means cooperation or help. Often used in official or semi-formal contexts when requesting someone's support.
Ma haraye muh huh-ruh-yay I am lost Essential phrase if you get lost. "Haraye" means lost. You can follow this with "bato dekhaunus" (please show me the way) to ask for directions back to your destination.
Hospital kaha cha? hos-pi-taal kuh-haa chuh Where is the hospital? Important phrase for emergencies. You can replace "hospital" with other locations like "police" (police station), "pharmacy" (ausadhi pasal), or any other place you need to find.
Malai biraami bhayo muh-lie bi-raa-mi bha-yo I am sick Use this when you're not feeling well. "Malai" means "to me," and "biraami" means sick. You can specify symptoms by adding words like "tauko dukhyo" (headache) or "pet dukhyo" (stomach ache).
Doctor chaahiyo doc-tor chaa-hi-yo I need a doctor An urgent phrase when medical attention is needed. "Chaahiyo" means "is needed." This will help communicate that you require medical assistance quickly.
Tauko dukhyo tow-ko dookh-yo My head hurts / Headache Describes a headache. "Tauko" means head and "dukhyo" means hurts or pained. You can use this pattern with other body parts: "pet dukhyo" (stomach ache), "khutta dukhyo" (leg hurts).
Pani chaahiyo paa-ni chaa-hi-yo I need water States you need water. More direct than "paani dinus" (please give water). "Chaahiyo" indicates necessity. You can replace "paani" with other items you need urgently.
Commands
Bistari bis-taa-ri Slowly / Carefully Can mean either slowly or carefully depending on context. You'll hear this when someone wants you to take your time or be cautious. "Bistari jaanus" means "go slowly/carefully."
Chitto chi-toh Quickly / Fast The opposite of bistari, meaning quickly or fast. "Chitto garnuhos" means "please do it quickly." Useful when you're in a hurry or need something done promptly.
Aaija aa-i-juh Come here An informal command asking someone to come. Used with friends, family, or subordinates. For a more polite version, use "aaunu hos" (please come).
Jaau jaa-oo Go / Let's go Can be a command "go" or a suggestion "let's go" depending on context and tone. Among friends, it's a common way to suggest leaving. More polite form is "jaanuhola" (shall we go?).
Basnus bus-noos Please sit Polite invitation to sit down. "Bas" means sit, and "nus" makes it respectful. Hosts commonly use this to invite guests to be seated. You can also say "yaha basnus" (sit here).
Khadai garnus kha-dye gar-noos Please eat Polite invitation to eat, commonly used by hosts. It's considered good hospitality to encourage guests to eat. You'll hear this often at meals in Nepali homes.
Piudai garnus pee-oo-dye gar-noos Please drink Polite invitation to drink, whether it's water, tea, or other beverages. Hosts use this to ensure guests are comfortable and well-served.

Namaste
nuh-muh-stay
Hello / Goodbye
This is the most common greeting in Nepal, used at any time of day. It's accompanied by pressing your palms together in front of your chest. The word literally means "I bow to the divine in you" and is appropriate in both formal and informal settings.
